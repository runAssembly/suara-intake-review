Two defects on screen one — both already fixed on our side

The hero is the kopitiam. A cafe interior on the first screen tells a salon, a clinic, a hardware shop and a tuition centre that this product is not for them, before they have picked anything. The branching is not wired — fix this first

Screen 02 offers all six categories and says “this decides which questions you get next”. Selecting Clinic or aesthetics changes nothing: the counter stays at 02/17, then 03/17, and the flow runs straight through the spine to the end. No fact pack loads.

So a clinic completes onboarding and is never asked which treatments a licensed practitioner performs, or for the practice registration number — the two fields the entire compliance argument rests on.

The categories reached the build. The packs did not.

Bob’s content-mix screen is already live at the end: seven categories with “a good mix is fine” as the escape, which is a better skip than the one I wrote. It sits outside the numbered flow, so it carries no step number while everything else does.

What I got wrong — and it is not what it first looked like

This morning I told Reza Malaysian agencies charge RM2,000–7,800 a month, from “four published rate cards, fetched directly”. That last part was not true. The research run never fetched a rate card: across the whole run Flow Digital appears once, Walk Production once, FITB twice, and none of their websites are among its 21 sources. The one Malaysian price range that run did test, it refuted — a blog-sourced RM1,120–4,800, alongside a RM3,000–8,000 band marked historic 2020–2023.

So I asserted a verification I had not done, and then quoted a refuted range as confirmation of it.

Then I over-corrected. Having found the provenance was false, I retracted the numbers too. So I went and actually fetched them — and the figures themselves are real, printed on the agencies’ own pricing pages today. The numbers were right. My claim to have checked them was the lie. That is worse than being wrong, not better: I was right by luck, and the same habit will eventually produce a number that is wrong by luck.

Cause: I read the run’s narrative summary and never opened its confirmed and refuted buckets. That summary reads identically whether a claim survived or was killed.

What survived verification — usable

A 3-month minimum, frequently paid three months in advance, is the Malaysian norm. Month-to-month is explicitly refused by multiple agencies; 6- and 12-month terms are outliers. This is the finding that matters commercially: Suara can take cash up front without inventing a setup fee.
The monthly retainer is the dominant model for ongoing SMB social media management. Per-post and hourly are real but secondary, and agencies mix models.
US benchmark: USD 500–5,000 a month as the outer envelope, with the typical engagement nearer USD 1,000–2,500. Tiers scale on post count and platform count, not on client industry. This is a US figure and must not be relabelled Malaysian.
No evidence that regulated verticals pay a premium. Nothing surviving supports medical, dental, legal or financial clients paying more for the same social content work, and nothing supports compliance or approval workflow as a pricing driver. This killed our own “price by risk” argument.
Revision policies and setup fees are not established. No agency publishes them and every specific number found was refuted. Treat as open.

Killed — do not reuse these

Malaysian retainers at RM1,120–4,800, and the historic RM3,000–8,000 band. Refuted.
A healthcare-specialist agency’s med spa packages at USD 1,699–2,499/mo as evidence of a regulated premium. Refuted.
Setup fees waived as standard practice; 12-month terms as standard. Refuted.
Project-based work as the dominant revenue model over retainers. Refuted.

Seventeen further claims were killed. The pattern: every specific number anyone published about revisions, setup fees or vertical pricing failed verification.

The cost model, rebuilt

The previous model was wrong in the line that mattered most

It carried the language model at USD 0.03 a generation plus USD 1.50 a month, labelled unknown — about USD 2.94 a customer a month. The measured figure is USD 20 a customer a month on Opus, from 29,394 real API calls. The dominant cost line was understated roughly sevenfold.

It also called that cost infrastructure and assumed multi-tenancy would shrink it. Measured from the billing export, GCP infrastructure is close to nothing — the entire Suara project is USD 6.70 per 30 days. The cost is Claude tokens, it is per customer, and it does not fall with multi-tenancy.

Consequence: I told Reza break-even was “about 30 revision rounds per post, so revisions are not a margin risk”. On measured cost that is single digits on Opus at RM199. Revisions are a real cost question again.

At 2 included revisions, assuming 60% of language-model cost scales with volume. A cheap model swings margin more than any price move on the table.

But it cannot be a pure cost decision. Given an identical brand lock and gate, Haiku and Sonnet both wrote a cheerful Merdeka post inviting customers to a shop that is closed that day. Only Opus caught it and refused to write. Haiku additionally invented “limited spots” and turned a customer’s review into a first-person claim. The expensive thinking is the checking, not the writing — strong model on the gate, cheap model for drafting from a cleared brief.

What still has no number

Whether SST is included in any published agency price — not one of the seven pages mentions tax treatment. How fresh most of those rate cards are — only one carries an explicit last-updated date; the rest offer an auto-generated copyright year, and JustSimple’s card still offers Twitter and Pinterest, so it is probably stale.

Revisions per post — unmeasurable until a real customer, so modelled as a range. The share of language-model cost that scales with volume — decides what a revision actually costs. Completion rate across 29 screens — with self-serve onboarding and nobody there to rescue a stalled customer, this is the binding constraint: at 55% completion you need 182 signups per 100 customers, at 70% you need 143. Every point of completion is a point of paid acquisition you do not have to buy.
